Ad Viewability & Invalid Traffic: The True Cost of Your CPM

Your dashboard reports a CPM. It does not report what you paid for a real impression that a human being actually saw. Here is the measurement standard, the arithmetic, and the 2026 data that closes the gap.

An advertiser pays a CPM. A publisher earns an eCPM. Both numbers are printed by the party that benefits from them, and both are computed over impressions served — not impressions seen by a human being in front of a screen.

The difference between those two denominators is the largest unmanaged cost line in digital advertising. It is not a rounding error. On a typical open-web display campaign in 2026, the gap between the reported CPM and the cost of one impression that was measurable, in view, human, and on a site a brand would actually choose runs 60% to 100%. This page shows how that gap is constructed, what the current measured benchmarks are, and how to audit it on your own account.

Why the CPM in Your Dashboard Is Not a Price

When a platform reports "CPM $5.00, 2,000,000 impressions," it is telling you the cost of an ad server event: a tag fired, a creative was delivered, and an impression counted. It is not telling you four things that determine whether the money worked:

  1. Whether the impression could be measured at all. Measurement scripts do not run on every served impression. The unmeasured tail is invisible in the reporting but fully priced.
  2. Whether the ad was on screen. The industry standard requires at least half the creative's pixels to be within the viewport for at least one continuous second. A large share of delivered impressions never meet it.
  3. Whether a human was present. Data-center traffic, crawlers, hidden iframes, and hijacked devices all generate valid-looking impression events.
  4. Whether the site was one you would choose. Low-quality "made for advertising" inventory frequently scores well on viewability, which is precisely why viewability alone is not a sufficient filter.
The one-sentence version: Reported CPM prices an impression event. Qualified CPM prices an outcome. Every layer between them is a discount you did not negotiate, and the industry's own 2026 measurement data says those layers are worth roughly half your media budget.

Everything below is the evidence for that sentence, in the order the deductions occur: the visibility standard, the measurement denominator, the traffic filter, and the supply-quality filter.

What "Viewable" Means — and Why Three Standards Give Three Bills

Ad viewability is a digital advertising metric that reports the proportion of ad impressions that were actually capable of being seen by a human, measured against a defined pixel-and-time threshold. The definition is not set by the vendor selling the ads.

The governing standard comes from the Media Rating Council (MRC), the nonprofit that accredits media measurement in the United States, and it has three parts rather than one:

MRC viewability thresholds by creative type, as implemented in platform and third-party measurement
CreativeThresholdTime in viewNotes
Display / banner≥ 50% of pixels≥ 1 continuous secondThe baseline most "viewability rate" figures use
In-stream video≥ 50% of pixels≥ 2 consecutive secondsUpdated from 1s to 2s for video
Large format (≥ 242,500 px)≥ 30% of pixels≥ 1 continuous secondAccommodates very large units where 50% is unrealistic

Sources: MRC Viewable Impression Guidelines; the same three thresholds are implemented in Google's Active View reporting (Understand viewability and Active View reporting metrics) and in Amazon Ads' Viewability guide. An impression that is clicked counts as viewable regardless of pixel time.

Three standards, three different invoices

Here is the part almost no explainer mentions: the MRC threshold is a floor, not the only rule in use. Different buyers apply different definitions to the same campaign, and each produces a different viewability rate and therefore a different effective price per thousand:

  • MRC "standard" — 50% of pixels for 1 second. This is what most platform dashboards report.
  • GroupM "strict" — 100% of pixels in view, audio on for video, and at least 50% of the creative's duration watched. Some large media agencies contract on this definition, and it can cut a reported viewability rate in half.
  • Vendor method differences — measurement polling intervals (100ms vs 200ms sampling), DOM-based geometry versus iframe-container inference, and whether the tag measures the creative or the container around it. Two accredited vendors on the same placement can disagree by several points.

Adobe's DSP documentation makes the split explicit by labelling its two modes "Standard" (MRC) and "Strict" (GroupM) side by side, and Microsoft's Xandr documentation devotes a page to why its own measured impression count exceeds what third parties report on the same inventory.

Sources: Adobe DSP placement-level pre-bid filters (Standard vs Strict definitions); Microsoft Xandr ad viewability methodology; Google Ad Manager Active View methodology.

Practical consequence: before you compare a viewability rate from a dashboard, a verification vendor, and an agency report, ask which threshold each used. If they differ, the three numbers are not three estimates of one quantity — they are three different quantities, and the vCPM price attached to each is different too.

The Denominator Trap: Served, Measurable, Viewable

Viewability rate is not calculated over the impressions you paid for. It is calculated over the impressions that could be measured:

Viewable rate = Viewable impressions / Measurable impressions

Measurable impressions are the subset where the measurement tag actually ran and could observe the viewport. That excludes creatives served to environments where tags fail, requests that time out, slots rendered after the user left, and inventory where the buyer's measurement was not eligible to run. Google reports this as the measurable rate, and its own documentation explains that viewable impressions and measurable impressions are separate reportable quantities.

Now look at what the industry's own transparency benchmark found in the unmeasured tail:

The trap in one line: non-measurable spend (15%) is larger than non-viewable spend (10%). Fixing your viewability rate does not touch the biggest visibility hole in the chain — and because viewability is reported over measurable impressions, a measurability gap silently flatters your viewability score while you keep paying for the unmeasured tail.

Some contracts do not count viewable impressions — they estimate them

The most under-discussed mechanic in this area is the gross-up. Where a portion of delivery cannot be measured, some contracts do not simply exclude it; they extrapolate the measured viewability rate onto the unmeasured remainder and bill against the extrapolation.

Amazon's own product terms illustrate the arithmetic with a worked example: of 1,000,000 delivered impressions, 900,000 are measurable and 800,000 of those are measured as viewable (88.9% of measurable). The contract then deems 888,888 impressions viewable — the 80% measured share applied across the full 1,111,111-impression base, so the unmeasured 100,000 impressions are credited as viewable at the measured rate.

Sources: Amazon Ads product and traffic-validation terms, Traffic validation (which also discloses a 30-day post-impression detection window) and Amazon Ads performance metric definitions. The 888,888 figure is Amazon's own worked example, not our estimate.

This is not fraud; it is disclosed contract arithmetic. But it means the viewability number on your invoice may be a modelled quantity rather than a counted one, and any "true CPM" calculation you build should state which basis it uses. We recommend the conservative basis: divide by counted viewable impressions only.

Invalid Traffic: GIVT vs SIVT, and Who Filters What

Invalid traffic (IVT) is ad impressions or clicks generated by machines, processes, or humans with no genuine intent to engage with the advertisement, and which platforms and measurement standards therefore exclude from billable or reportable delivery. IVT splits into two categories with very different economics, and almost every "ad fraud" number you have read conflates them.

The two IVT classes, who is expected to filter them, and how the filtering shows up in your bill
ClassTypical sourcesWho filters itCost behaviour
GIVT (generally invalid)Data-centre traffic, known bots and spiders, non-browser user agents, prefetch/prerender events, unidentified crawlersPlatforms filter it automatically; it is excluded from billing by defaultLow visible cost. Cheap to detect, so it is a small share of measured spend
SIVT (sophisticated invalid)Device and app-ID spoofing, domain spoofing, hidden and stacked iframes, pixel flooding, click injection and hijacking, incentivised traffic, bot farms on real devices, falsified geolocation, cookie stuffing, hijacked devicesRequires separate detection, contractual terms, and often a third-party vendorThe expensive half. Detection lags delivery by weeks, so recovery is contractual, not automatic

Sources: MRC Invalid Traffic Filtering Guidelines (2021) for the GIVT/SIVT definitions; category lists as published in Oracle Marketing Cloud measurement's IVT glossary and Google's invalid traffic refund documentation. Google Ad Manager's metrics table notes that viewable totals are reported "net of compliant GIVT filtration, as well as some SIVT filtering techniques" — i.e. the filter applied is partial.

The asymmetry is the whole story. GIVT is cheap to catch, so everybody catches it, and the reported number is small. SIVT is expensive to catch, requires post-hoc analysis, and is usually only visible if you are paying a verification vendor and asking for the right report. Pixalate, which publishes one of the more granular IVT taxonomies, enumerated 41 distinct IVT types when it launched its Clawback reporting in November 2025 — 30 of them SIVT (app spoofing, device-ID stuffing, hidden IPs) and 11 GIVT (data-centre, IAB crawlers, TOR nodes) — with a lookback window of up to one year.

Sources: Pixalate Clawback Report launch, November 2025 (41 IVT types, 30 SIVT / 11 GIVT, up to 12-month lookback, SHA-256 hashed and signed evidence for clawback negotiation).

What 2026 added: machine-generated traffic that is not a bot in the classic sense

Two 2025–2026 developments changed the mix. First, AI crawlers and scrapers now generate impression events at scale: DoubleVerify reported an 86% increase in IVT driven by AI crawlers and scrapers in January 2025. Second, AI-generated "slop" inventory is being measured as supply rather than as traffic. In work presented with TAG and ANA in mid-2026, AI-slop inventory showed an IVT rate of 0.05% versus 0.32% for clean supply — that is, the fraudulent traffic on it is low, because the fraud is in the content, not the click.

Sources: DoubleVerify, January 2025 (AI crawler/scraper IVT growth, reported by Business Times); TAG, ANA and Fiducia measurement work on AI-generated inventory, July 2026. Pangram's independent research estimated roughly 60,000 AI-generated "news" articles published in a single day, which is the supply-side origin of the category.

Read the gap, not the average. The same market measures 9.96% IVT when buys are unfiltered and under 1% when they run through certified channels. That is not a debate about how much fraud exists; it is a measurement of what filtering is worth. If your programmatic buys have no pre-bid traffic-quality filter, the low number in your vendor's dashboard is a statement about what they looked for, not about what was there.

Why the giant dollar figures cannot be added up

You will see global losses quoted at $41B, $63B, $84B, $125B and higher for overlapping years. They are not competing estimates of one quantity; they measure different things:

  • $41B global / >$12.5B US — Media Trust (2026) counts malware, scams and ad-borne fraud losses, a crime-and-malware basis.
  • $63B in 2025 — Lunio's Global Invalid Traffic Report counts invalid traffic across search, social and affiliate, including spam leads and low-intent clicks, a traffic-quality basis.
  • $84B–$172B — the HUMAN Security forecast line counts fraud across ecommerce, apps and advertising on a projected-loss basis.
  • $22B wasted of $88B audited — ANA's supply-chain study counts programmatic media waste inside audited marketer accounts, the narrowest and most defensible basis for a media buyer.

Sources: Media Trust, reported March 2026; Lunio Global Invalid Traffic Report 2026 (2025 measurement year); HUMAN Security annual ad fraud forecast; ANA Programmatic Media Supply Chain Transparency Study, December 2023 (with PwC US, TAG and Kroll; Sept 2022–Jan 2023 window; 3.55 billion impressions and $123M spend audited).

For anyone calculating what their CPM really costs, only the last basis is usable, because it is the only one measured inside a buyer's own accounts. That is the number we build the ladder on below.

Made-for-Advertising Supply: Why Viewability Alone Fails

A "made for advertising" (MFA) site is a web property created primarily to host advertising rather than to serve readers, typically dependent on paid traffic, monetised at very high ad density with rapid slot refresh, and performing well on engagement metrics that do not translate into conversions.

The important part is not the definition. It is what these sites do to your filters. Integral Ad Science, in the announcement of its own MFA detection technology, described MFA sites as properties optimised to perform well against traditional verification metrics such as viewability. Jounce Media, whose three-part MFA criteria are the industry reference, has made the same point: MFA inventory scores highly on vanity KPIs like viewability and video completion while producing no purchase impact.

Sources: Integral Ad Science MFA detection announcement, October 2023; Jounce Media MFA criteria (paid-traffic dependence, aggressive monetisation, vanity-KPI performance), as summarised in Pangram's research on AI-generated news supply.

This is the logical core of the whole problem. If the worst inventory in the market is engineered to pass the viewability test, then subtracting non-viewable impressions does not get you to real humans on real content. Viewability is necessary and insufficient — which is why the ladder below applies a separate MFA deduction rather than treating viewability as the quality filter.

Measured MFA exposure is small in aggregate and enormous in the wrong places. ANA's benchmark programme put MFA at 0.5% of spend in Q3 2025, rising to 1.1% in Q1 2026 as ANA members' exposure doubled quarter-on-quarter from 0.6%. The earlier and much larger ANA study found 21% of measured impressions and 15% of media dollars (about $13B) landing on MFA sites, and reported that the average audited campaign ran on roughly 44,000 domains while about two-thirds of display delivery came from the top 500 of them.

Sources: ANA Programmatic Transparency Benchmark Q3 2025 and Q1 2026 findings; ANA Programmatic Media Supply Chain Transparency Study, December 2023 (audited $88B of programmatic spend with PwC US, TAG and Kroll). The 21% figure and the 0.5–1.1% figures are not contradictory: they use different populations (all measured impressions in a 2022–23 audit vs ANA members' filtered buys in 2025–26) and different detection thresholds.

The device split from IAS is the practical takeaway: MFA exposure runs 2.0% on mobile web versus 0.5% on desktop, a four-fold difference, with mobile web carrying roughly 72% of total MFA exposure despite being under half of impressions. If you are buying open-web display without placement-level reporting, mobile web is where your money is going quietly.

The Waste Ladder: From Booked CPM to Qualified CPM

Now assemble the deductions. This is the arithmetic that turns a reported CPM into a cost per qualified impression — one that was measurable, in view, human, and not on industrial advertising-only supply.

Qualified impressions = Served × Measurable rate × Viewable rate (of measurable) × (1 − IVT) × (1 − MFA)
Qualified CPM = Spend / Qualified impressions × 1,000

Worked example, using a $10,000 buy of 2,000,000 impressions (a reported CPM of $5.00) and the 2026 measured medians used throughout this page:

The headline number is $8.84 for a real impression against a reported $5.00. Two-thirds of the inflation comes from one place: the visibility gap, not the fraud gap. Fraud and supply-quality filters are worth having, but the money is in measurability and viewability.

That ordering matches the industry's own conclusion. ANA's benchmark reports a TrueCPM metric on exactly this basis, and the spread it measures between better and worse managed accounts is large: $7.46 for higher-performing advertisers versus $19.04 for lower performers in the Q1–Q2 2026 programme. Same market, same auction, a 2.6× difference in what a real impression costs.

Why Cheap CPMs Are the Fraud Magnet

The most expensive-looking number in a programmatic report is usually the cheapest place to lose money. In ANA's benchmark programme, the headline programmatic CPM paid by participating marketers fell from $5.55 to $4.42 between the Q1 2026 and later cuts, while the TrueAdSpend Index sat at 43.3%. CPMs went down; the share of that CPM reaching a real, in-view human did not go up proportionally.

The mechanism is straightforward. Inventory is priced by what the seller can prove about it, not by what the buyer needs. When a supply path has no meaningful viewability or traffic-quality filter attached, it clears at a discount — and the discount exists because someone, somewhere, measured the reason for it.

Refunds, Make-Goods and the 10% Discrepancy Rule

Nothing on this page is recovered automatically. Deductions for invalid traffic arrive through contract mechanics, and if you do not know which mechanism applies to your buys, you are accepting a default of zero recovery.

How the money actually comes back, by channel and contract term
MechanismHow it worksWhere it is documented

Four clauses to add to your next insertion order

  1. Named controlling measurement vendor with the exact threshold definition (MRC standard, or strict/GroupM if that is what you are buying to) and the discrepancy tolerance.
  2. Traffic-quality warranty referencing a maximum SIVT rate, with a clawback or make-good remedy and a stated lookback window.
  3. Measurability commitment — a minimum share of impressions that must be measurable by the controlling vendor. Almost nobody negotiates this, and it is the 15% line item.
  4. Supply exclusion list for MFA and previously flagged domains, with the right to audit placement-level delivery reports.

ads.txt and sellers.json: What Attestation Cannot Do

Four specifications exist to make programmatic supply paths legible. They are sequential, and each answers a different question:

The commercial reading is narrow but real: attestation stops domain and inventory spoofing, which is one SIVT technique, and it correlates with materially better traffic quality (the 78% Pixalate gap). It does nothing about viewability, ad clutter, MFA content, or bots running on real devices. A campaign can be 100% ads.txt-clean and still have half its spend land in the unmeasurable tail.

We ran our own crawl of publisher ads.txt files to test how much of this is theoretical — including how many files fail to parse cleanly and how many seller IDs named in them do not appear in the corresponding exchange's sellers.json. How to Audit Your Own Campaign in Six Steps

This is the workflow we use on any open-web display or video buy. It takes about an hour with placement-level exports and no vendor access beyond what your own account already reports.

  1. Pull the measurable rate first, not the viewability rate. In Google Ads this is the measurable cost and measurable-rate columns in the viewability report; in Ad Manager it is viewable versus measurable impressions. If measurable impressions are well below served impressions, you have found your largest hole before you start.
  2. Read the viewable and non-viewable impression distribution, not just the rate. A 68% rate can be 68% on every placement or 95% on two placements and 30% everywhere else. The distribution is the actionable object.
  3. Sort spend, not impressions, by placement. Export placement-level (URL / app ID) spend and viewable impressions, then compute cost per viewable impression per placement. This is where the cheap-CPM problem becomes visible: the cheapest CPMs are usually the most expensive viewable CPMs.
  4. Cross-check the platform number against the controlling vendor's number. Compute the discrepancy. Under 10% is normal. Above 10% is a contractual conversation, and you should know which measurement is controlling before you have it.
  5. Ask which IVT class each report covers. Some reports show a single blended "fraud" rate; others split GIVT and SIVT and show what was filtered pre-bid versus detected post-bid. A 0.4% fraud rate with no class breakdown is not a result you can act on.
  6. Flag MFA and clutter separately. Most vendors now score MFA exposure and ad clutter as distinct metrics. Because MFA inventory is engineered to pass viewability, this is the only step in the audit that catches it.

The Publisher Mirror: Same Math, Inverted

If you run a site, the same ladder explains why your RPM moves without your traffic changing. Buyers increasingly pay on viewable and qualified terms, so a publisher's realised revenue per thousand served requests is the advertiser's cost per qualified impression seen from the other side.

Realisation rate = Billed eCPM on served requests / Qualified-impression eCPM

Three publisher-side factors show up in that ratio more than ad placement tweaks do:

  • Slot position and lazy-load behaviour. A unit that renders below the fold on a page most visitors leave after two paragraphs is sold into a discount, not a premium. This is the single biggest lever, and it is the same arithmetic as improving your CPM from the sell side.
  • Ad clutter. IAS tracked open-web ad clutter worsening from 0.3% to 0.8% across 2025. More units per page raises gross revenue and lowers viewability per unit; the net can be negative once buyers price visibility.
  • Attestation hygiene. A malformed or missing ads.txt line removes you from demand that filters on it. That is a direct auction-participation problem, not a documentation problem.

Sources: IAS 21st Media Quality Report (ad clutter trend); AdSense guidance on ads.txt configuration and crawl errors. Publisher-side benchmark data on this site: Average RPM by Platform and How to Calculate Advertising CPM Effectively.

Viewability Is Not Attention

The last honest caveat in this article: even a perfectly qualified impression is not an attended one. The MRC threshold asks whether pixels were on screen, not whether a person looked.

Peer-reviewed work published in the Journal of Advertising Research in October 2025 examined the 1-second metric directly and found it fails asymmetrically: it is better at identifying true positives (people who were attending) than at identifying inattention. In other words, the standard errs in the seller's favour, and the error is not random noise.

The commercial response has been the migration toward attention metrics. In IAS's published attention experiments, an attention-optimised Kimberly-Clark campaign reported a 313% household sales lift and 78% higher ROAS versus its display baseline, with a 56% higher attention score at a 76% lower CPC. Treat those as vendor-reported case-study outcomes rather than a market average — but note that the direction is consistent with everything above: what you are really buying is not an impression, and not even a viewable impression.

Sources: Journal of Advertising Research, October 2025, on the discriminative validity of the one-second viewability threshold; Integral Ad Science attention-metric case study (Kimberly-Clark), vendor-reported. Google's average time-in-view metric provides the in-platform version of the same idea.

Our Take

These are our judgements, not the sources' conclusions. We think they are the parts of this topic most guides get wrong.

1. The measurability hole is the most under-priced risk in programmatic. Everybody argues about fraud; almost nobody negotiates measurability. ANA's own line items put non-measurable spend above non-viewable spend, and no dashboard metric is dedicated to it. If you only fix one thing on this list, fix the denominator.

2. Viewability targets are a management illusion. A 68% global display rate is not a performance number for a specific campaign; it is a description of a market. Setting an internal target of "80% viewability" mostly incentivises buying on placements where the measurement is generous. Compare placements against each other on cost per viewable impression instead, and stop reporting the rate itself.

3. Fraud percentages are marketing until you know the basis. The same year can honestly produce "<1%" and "23%" depending on whether the vendor is measuring filtered display, unfiltered open exchange, or mobile in-app. We have deliberately kept the bases separate in the tables above rather than blending them into one impressive number, because blended fraud statistics are the least useful artefact in this industry.

4. MFA is the reason this problem survived a decade of viewability enforcement. The market built a visibility filter, and supply reorganised to pass it. Any quality metric that is cheap to satisfy at the pixel level will be satisfied at the pixel level. This is why we would weight contract clauses toward supply exclusion and audit rights rather than toward higher viewability floors.

5. Cheap CPMs deserve an argument, not a budget. The instinct to buy more impressions when prices fall is exactly backwards on the open exchange. ANA's data shows headline CPMs falling while working-media share stayed around 43–45%. If a price is low, the seller is telling you something; the only question is whether you are measuring what they are hiding.

6. Small advertisers should not buy the open exchange at all. The audit workflow above assumes placement-level reporting, a verification vendor, and negotiating leverage. Most of our readers have none of those. For budgets under roughly $10k/month, walled-garden or curated inventory with platform-side filtering is a better economic choice than cheap open supply you cannot audit — and the CPM you see there is much closer to the CPM you pay.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good viewability rate in 2026?

Against measured market data, roughly 68% for display and roughly 80% for video are the 2026 open-web averages, so "good" means materially above those for the same format, device and market. Anything quoted as a universal target — "70% is great, 40% works too" — is unsourced. Compare placements on cost per viewable impression instead of comparing rates.

How do you calculate cost per viewable impression?

Divide spend by viewable impressions and multiply by 1,000. The critical detail is that viewable impressions are counted over measurable impressions, so if you want a number comparable to your reported CPM, you must also decide what happens to the unmeasurable remainder — exclude it (conservative) or extrapolate the measured rate onto it (what some contracts do).

What is the difference between GIVT and SIVT?

GIVT is invalid traffic that is easy to identify by general criteria — data-centre IPs, known bots and crawlers, non-browser user agents — and platforms filter it automatically at no cost to you. SIVT is deliberately disguised invalid traffic — spoofed apps and domains, hidden or stacked iframes, bot farms on real devices — which requires separate detection, contractual terms, and post-hoc recovery.

Does Google refund invalid traffic?

For Display & Video 360, Google documents that its exchange partners agreed to refund IVT, and the refund appears as an adjustment line item on settlement reports rather than in the campaign dashboard. On AdSense, the flow runs the other way: Google can withhold publisher earnings for invalid activity, up to the total earnings of the prior 60 days. Automatic GIVT filtration happens without a claim in either case.

Why is my viewability rate high but my results bad?

Three common causes: the rate is measured over a small measurable subset while most spend sits in the unmeasurable tail; the inventory is made-for-advertising supply, which is engineered to score well on viewability; or the impressions cleared the one-second pixel threshold with almost no time in view. Check measurable rate, MFA exposure and average time in view before touching creative.

Is viewability a vanity metric?

It is a necessary but insufficient one. It was designed as a floor against invisible ad slots, and it works for that. It fails as a proxy for attention — the one-second threshold has been shown to err in the seller's favour — and it fails entirely against supply that optimises for it. Use it as a filter, not as a goal.

Conclusion

The CPM in your dashboard is a price per ad event. The number that matters is a price per impression that was measurable, in view, human, and on inventory you would choose. On 2026 measured medians, that transformation takes a $5.00 reported CPM to roughly $8.84, and ANA's own benchmark shows well-managed accounts paying $7.46 where poorly managed ones pay $19.04 for the same unit.

The gap is not mysterious and it is not evenly spread. Most of it is the visibility denominator — unmeasurable and non-viewable delivery — and the remainder is concentrated in mobile web open exchange supply. Filters, contracts and audits recover a real share of it; higher viewability floors alone do not.

Methodology and limitations

  • Bases are not comparable. Impression-level flagged rates, share-of-spend decompositions, and global loss estimates measure different populations. We have not averaged across them, and neither should you.
  • Vendor measurements are self-interested. Every fraud and viewability figure cited here comes from a company that sells the filter that improves it. The protected-versus-unprotected gaps are useful precisely because they are the vendor's own comparison of no-filter delivery.
  • Independence assumption. Our waste-ladder multiplication assumes each layer is independent. In practice some IVT is also non-viewable, so the true qualified count is slightly lower and our qualified CPM is a conservative floor.
  • Open web only. ANA's Q3 2025 decomposition excludes CTV, and CTV IVT measures an order of magnitude higher. Do not apply these rates to a CTV buy.
