How to Calculate RPM on YouTube: Step-by-Step Guide (2026)

Master YouTube's most important earnings metric with real examples, niche benchmarks, and actionable optimization tips.

If you are a YouTube creator, RPM (Revenue Per Mille) is arguably the single most important metric for understanding how much money you make. While CPM tells you what advertisers pay, RPM tells you what actually lands in your pocket per 1,000 views. In this guide, we walk through exactly how to calculate RPM on YouTube, what factors influence it, and how to improve it.

What Is YouTube RPM?

YouTube RPM measures the total revenue a creator earns per 1,000 video views. Unlike CPM, which only reflects what advertisers pay, RPM encompasses all revenue sources flowing to the creator. This makes RPM a more comprehensive measure of your actual earnings power on the platform.

RPM is the metric YouTube displays in your YouTube Studio analytics, and it is the number most creators reference when discussing their YouTube income. When a YouTuber says "I make $7 per 1,000 views," they are talking about RPM.

The YouTube RPM Formula

The formula for calculating YouTube RPM is straightforward:

RPM = (Total Revenue / Total Views) × 1,000

For example, if your video earned $150 in total revenue and received 30,000 views:

RPM = ($150 / 30,000) × 1,000 = $5.00

This means you earned $5 for every 1,000 views that video received.

Important: YouTube's RPM uses total views (not just monetized playbacks), which makes it different from how some other platforms calculate similar metrics. This means your RPM will always be lower than your ad CPM because not every view generates ad revenue.

What Revenue Is Included in RPM?

YouTube RPM includes revenue from multiple sources, not just ads:

  • Ad revenue: Money from pre-roll, mid-roll, post-roll, and display ads shown on your videos. This is typically the largest component.
  • YouTube Premium revenue: A share of subscription fees from YouTube Premium members who watch your content.
  • Super Chat and Super Stickers: Payments from viewers during live streams for highlighted messages.
  • Channel memberships: Monthly subscription fees from members who pay for exclusive perks.
  • Super Thanks: One-time tips from viewers on individual videos.
  • YouTube Shopping: Revenue from affiliate product links integrated into your videos.

Because RPM includes all these sources, a creator with strong membership and Super Chat revenue might have a significantly higher RPM than a creator who relies solely on ad revenue, even if their ad CPMs are similar.

Step-by-Step Calculation Example

Let's walk through a complete example. Suppose you run a personal finance channel and in the month of January 2026, your channel performance looks like this:

Step 1: Gather Your Revenue Data

  • Ad revenue: $3,200
  • YouTube Premium revenue: $400
  • Channel memberships: $600
  • Super Chat: $200
  • Super Thanks: $100
  • Total Revenue: $4,500

Step 2: Get Your Total Views

Your videos received a combined 600,000 views in January.

Step 3: Apply the Formula

RPM = ($4,500 / 600,000) × 1,000 = $7.50

Step 4: Interpret the Result

Your RPM of $7.50 means you earned $7.50 for every 1,000 views. This is above the average for finance channels ($5-$10 RPM), suggesting your audience is well-monetized. If you project this RPM across 1 million views, you would earn approximately $7,500 per month.

YouTube's Revenue Share Model

Understanding YouTube's revenue share is essential for understanding why your RPM is lower than the CPM advertisers pay:

  • Long-form videos: Creators receive 55% of ad revenue. YouTube keeps 45%.
  • YouTube Shorts: Creators receive 45% of ad revenue from the Shorts ad pool. YouTube keeps 55%.
  • Channel memberships: Creators receive 70% (YouTube keeps 30%).
  • Super Chat/Super Thanks: Creators receive 70% (YouTube keeps 30%).
Revenue Share Impact: If an advertiser pays a $10 CPM on your long-form video, your share of the ad revenue is $5.50 (55%). Your RPM will be even lower than $5.50 because not every view is monetized with ads (due to ad blockers, YouTube Premium viewers, skipped ads, etc.).

How to Find Your RPM in YouTube Studio

Finding your RPM in YouTube Studio is simple:

  1. Sign in to YouTube Studio (studio.youtube.com)
  2. Click on Analytics in the left sidebar
  3. Select the Revenue tab
  4. Your RPM is displayed at the top of the page alongside total revenue
  5. You can adjust the date range to see RPM over different periods
  6. To see RPM for individual videos, go to Content and sort by revenue

You can also see estimated RPM broken down by traffic source, geography, and device type in the Advanced Mode analytics.

Tips to Increase Your YouTube RPM

  1. Target high-CPM niches: Finance, tech, and business content attracts premium advertisers.
  2. Create longer videos (8+ minutes): Videos over 8 minutes can include mid-roll ads, which significantly increase revenue per view.
  3. Focus on Tier 1 countries: Viewers from the US, UK, Canada, and Australia generate 3-10x higher CPMs than viewers from developing countries.
  4. Improve audience retention: Higher retention means more ad impressions per view, especially mid-roll ads.
  5. Enable all revenue features: Turn on memberships, Super Chat, Super Thanks, and Shopping to diversify beyond ads.
  6. Upload consistently: Channels that upload regularly build loyal audiences that generate higher engagement and RPM.
  7. Optimize for advertiser-friendly topics: Content about products, services, and high-value industries attracts more advertisers.
  8. Post during Q4: Advertising budgets peak in October-December, pushing CPMs (and therefore RPM) significantly higher.

Shorts vs. Long-Form RPM

The difference between YouTube Shorts RPM and long-form RPM is enormous:

  • Long-form RPM: Typically $2 - $28 depending on niche
  • Shorts RPM: Typically $0.01 - $0.06 per 1,000 views

This massive gap exists because Shorts have lower ad load, a different revenue sharing model (45% to creators), and ads are served between videos in the Shorts feed rather than within individual videos. A video with 1 million Shorts views might earn $10-$60, while a long-form video with the same views could earn $3,000-$15,000.

If your goal is to maximize revenue, prioritize long-form content. Use Shorts as a discovery tool to drive viewers to your long-form videos where the real monetization happens.

Conclusion

Calculating RPM on YouTube is simple: divide your total revenue by total views, then multiply by 1,000. But optimizing your RPM is an ongoing process that involves content strategy, audience targeting, and revenue diversification. Track your RPM regularly in YouTube Studio, compare it against niche benchmarks, and use the tips above to steadily increase your earnings per view.
